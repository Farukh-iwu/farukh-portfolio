Artifact Information

Artifact Title

What It Takes to Train an LLM: Process, Resources, and Costs

Introduction

Headlines quote single numbers for what a model "cost," but those numbers measure different things. This infographic breaks training into its stages and its resources so that each published figure can be read in context.

Description

A one-page infographic with a six-stage pipeline diagram, five resource cards, three charts, a cost explainer, and a model comparison table built from technical reports, model cards, and peer-reviewed estimates.

Objective

Illustrate the key steps from data collection to deployment, explain the primary resource categories and their costs, and give specific, sourced examples for prominent generative AI models.

Process

Read the GPT-3, GPT-4, Llama 2, Llama 3, and DeepSeek-V3 reports, the Llama 3.1 model card, and cost and carbon studies; logged every figure with its page; then designed the pipeline, charts, and table around what was actually published.

Tools and Tech Used

Primary research papers and model cards for content; HTML, CSS, inline SVG icons, and vanilla JavaScript for the charts and tooltips; a colorblind-safety check on the chart palette; GitHub Pages for publishing.

Value Proposition of the Artifact

A reader can see where the money, power, and time go in an LLM project, and can tell an amortized cost estimate apart from a hardware purchase price or a single-run rental figure.

Unique Value

Every number names what it measures and who reported it, and the table marks what developers chose not to disclose. Gaps are shown as gaps, not filled with guesses.

Relevance

Useful for engineers deciding whether to pre-train, fine-tune, or use an API; for managers budgeting AI work; and for anyone comparing claims about cheap or expensive models.

References

13 sources: technical reports from OpenAI, Meta, and DeepSeek; the Llama 3.1 model card; Epoch AI's cost study; carbon studies by Google and BigScience researchers; and course readings. Full list below.

🔁 The Training Pipeline: Six Stages

Every model in this artifact follows the same broad path. The chips under each stage show which resources it draws on; an outlined chip marks the dominant one.

Data Compute Energy Time People
  1. 1

    Data Collection

    Preparation

    Gather web pages, books, code, and reference text, plus licensed data where a developer pays for access.

    Data People
    GPT-3: filtered Common Crawl supplied 410B tokens and 60% of the training mix, with the rest from WebText2, two book corpora, and Wikipedia (Brown et al., 2020, p. 9). GPT-4 used public and licensed data (OpenAI, 2023, p. 1).
  2. 2

    Cleaning and Tokenization

    Preparation

    Remove duplicates, personal data, and unsafe content, balance the mix of topics, then split text into tokens the model can read.

    Data Compute People
    Llama 3: de-duplicated at the URL, document, and line level and filtered out sites heavy in personal information (Grattafiori et al., 2024, pp. 4-5). The final mix was about 50% general knowledge, 25% math and reasoning, 17% code, and 8% multilingual (p. 6).
  3. 3

    Pre-training

    Core training

    The model learns to predict the next token across trillions of examples. This is where most of the compute, energy, and calendar time goes.

    Compute Energy Time
    Llama 3 405B: up to 16,000 H100 GPUs and 3.8 × 1025 floating point operations (Grattafiori et al., 2024, pp. 1, 9). DeepSeek-V3: 2.66M of its 2.79M GPU hours went to pre-training (DeepSeek-AI, 2024, p. 5).
  4. 4

    Fine-tuning and Alignment

    Post-training

    Supervised examples teach the model to follow instructions; human preference rankings then steer it toward helpful, safe answers.

    People Data Compute
    InstructGPT: three steps (supervised fine-tuning, a reward model, then reinforcement learning) with labels from about 40 contractors (Ouyang et al., 2022, pp. 3, 7). Llama 3 used fine-tuning, rejection sampling, and direct preference optimization (Grattafiori et al., 2024, p. 2).
  5. 5

    Evaluation and Safety Testing

    Post-training

    Benchmarks measure capability; red teams and domain experts probe for harmful or unreliable behavior before release.

    People Time
    GPT-4: more than 50 experts in fields such as cybersecurity and biorisk tested the model adversarially (OpenAI, 2023, p. 12). Meta released Llama Guard 3 alongside Llama 3 to filter inputs and outputs (Grattafiori et al., 2024, p. 1).
  6. 6

    Deployment and Inference

    Operation

    The model is served through an API or released as open weights. Every user request now runs the model, so compute and energy costs continue after training ends.

    Compute Energy
    Ongoing cost: Patterson et al. (2021, p. 2) report that most companies spend more energy serving models than training them, citing an NVIDIA estimate that 80-90% of ML workload is inference.

Stages 1-3 build a base model that can continue text; stages 4-5 turn it into an assistant. At its core, as Wolfram (2023, "It's Just Adding One Word at a Time" section) explains, the model tries to produce a "reasonable continuation" of the text so far.

🧩 Five Resource Categories

The assignment names four resources: data, compute, energy, and time. The cost research adds a fifth that is easy to miss: the people who build the model.

Data

300B → 15.6T

Training tokens, GPT-3 (2020) to Llama 3 405B (2024)

Data costs come from licensing, as with the third-party data used for GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023, p. 1), and from the engineering work of cleaning and filtering.

Effect on performance: Hoffmann et al. (2022, pp. 1-2) found that model size and training tokens should grow together. Their 70B Chinchilla, trained on 1.4T tokens, used the same compute as the 280B Gopher and outperformed it. Since then, token counts have grown faster than parameter counts.

Compute

65%

Share of GPT-4's development cost spent on hardware (Cottier et al., 2024, p. 9)

Accelerator chips are the largest hardware cost. DeepSeek-V3 trained on a cluster of 2,048 H800 GPUs (DeepSeek-AI, 2024, p. 11); Llama 3 used up to 16,000 H100s.

Effect on cost: For GPT-4, chips were 32% of amortized development cost, other server parts 21%, and cluster networking 12% (Cottier et al., 2024, p. 9). Buying that hardware outright was estimated at $800M (p. 6).

Energy

1,287 MWh

Estimated electricity to train GPT-3, producing 552 tCO2e (Patterson et al., 2021, p. 7)

Energy was only 2-6% of total cost for frontier models (Cottier et al., 2024, p. 1), but power supply is becoming a limit: Gemini Ultra's training cluster was estimated at about 35 MW (p. 19).

Grid matters: BLOOM used 433 MWh on a French grid at 57 gCO2eq/kWh and emitted 24.7 t from training power (Luccioni et al., 2022, pp. 2, 4). Llama 3 training caused power swings of tens of megawatts across the data center (Grattafiori et al., 2024, p. 14).

Time

14.8 → 118 days

GPT-3 on 10,000 V100s (Patterson et al., 2021, p. 6); BLOOM on A100s (Luccioni et al., 2022, p. 2)

DeepSeek-V3's pre-training finished in under two months (DeepSeek-AI, 2024, p. 5). Long runs tie up hardware and push back release dates.

Time is also risk: in a 54-day stretch of Llama 3 pre-training there were 466 job interruptions, 419 of them unplanned and about 78% traced to hardware (Grattafiori et al., 2024, p. 13).

People

29-49%

R&D staff share of total development cost for GPT-3, OPT-175B, GPT-4, and Gemini Ultra

Reported contributors grew from 25 on GPT-3 to 284 on GPT-4 (Cottier et al., 2024, pp. 7, 10). Alignment adds labelers and red-teamers on top.

Effect on performance: human feedback can matter more than size. Labelers preferred answers from the 1.3B InstructGPT model to those of the 175B GPT-3 (Ouyang et al., 2022, p. 1).

💡 Key Takeaways

Pre-training is the compute bill; post-training is the people bill

DeepSeek-V3 spent about 96% of its GPU hours on pre-training and under 1% on post-training. Alignment and safety testing are cheap in GPU time but depend on labelers, experts, and staff, which is where the 29-49% staff share comes from.

Ask what a cost figure measures

The $5.6M DeepSeek-V3 figure is a rental price for one final run. The $40M GPT-4 figure is an amortized estimate of the run. The $800M figure is hardware capital. None of the three includes the full cost of research and staff.

Energy is a small cost but a large footprint

Energy was 2-6% of cost, yet emissions range from 24.7 t to 8,930 t. Run size and grid carbon intensity account for much of that range: BLOOM's low figure reflects a low-carbon French grid as well as a smaller run.

Openness varies widely

Meta and DeepSeek published GPU hours and token counts. OpenAI's GPT-4 report states it contains no details on model size, hardware, compute, or dataset construction (OpenAI, 2023, p. 1). Most cost figures for closed models are outside estimates.

🗃️ How the Content Is Classified

The artifact sorts every fact along three axes. Each axis answers a different question a reader brings to an LLM project.

Axis 1: Stage (when?)

Six stages grouped into four phases: preparation, core training, post-training, and operation. Facts were sorted by the step of the model's life they describe. Evaluation is its own stage because the GPT-4 report treats expert adversarial testing as separate work, and deployment is included because inference keeps consuming compute after training ends.

Axis 2: Resource (what is consumed?)

The assignment names data, compute, energy, and time. People became a fifth category after the cost study showed staff at 29-49% of development cost, a share as large as the chips. Each stage is marked with one primary resource and any secondary ones, based on where the sources report the largest spending.

Axis 3: Cost type and disclosure (what does the number mean?)

Dollar figures are labeled as amortized cost, hardware purchase, final-run rental, developer statement, or trend. Missing values are split into "not disclosed" (the developer withheld it) and "not reported" (absent from the sources reviewed), because those are different kinds of gaps.

Hard calls

Tokenization was grouped with cleaning, since both prepare data before training. Energy and compute stay separate even though one drives the other: energy has its own costs and a carbon footprint that depends on the grid, not on the chips.

🎨 Design Rationale

One color per resource

Data, compute, energy, time, and people each keep one color across the pipeline chips, resource cards, and charts. The five colors were checked for colorblind separation, and every bar also carries a text label, so color is never the only cue.

Process first, numbers second

The pipeline comes before any figures so readers know where a number belongs before they see it. Each stage card ends with a real example from a named model.

Charts chosen by the question

A 100% stacked bar answers "what share goes where"; horizontal bars compare a single quantity across models. Only figures from the same study share a stacked chart, and every chart has a table view.

Honest gaps

Undisclosed values stay marked as undisclosed instead of being filled with rumored numbers, because a believable but unsourced figure would mislead the reader.

💭 Professional Reflection

Reading the technical reports side by side showed that a "training cost" can mean a rental price, an amortized estimate, or a hardware purchase, and that these can differ by twenty times for the same model, as GPT-4's $40M and $800M estimates do. The habit I took from this project is asking what a figure measures before comparing it with another.

The hardest design problem was missing data. The closed models I most wanted to compare publish the least, and filling the gaps with figures from news articles would have made the infographic look complete but less trustworthy. I chose to show the gaps as gaps and to put estimates from a single study in the same chart only when they used the same method.

The research tied directly to this workshop's topics. Pre-training needs no human labels because the next token in the text is the target, while fine-tuning is supervised and RLHF adds a learned reward model. GPT-3's authors warned that web data can contain benchmark test sets and built tools to measure that contamination, which is the training-versus-testing data problem at a very large scale. They also sampled their best datasets two to three times, accepting "a small amount of overfitting in exchange for higher quality training data" (Brown et al., 2020, p. 8). Meta treated model size as a parameter to tune: it trained models from 40M to 16B parameters on small budgets, then used the fitted curve to choose a size for the full run (Grattafiori et al., 2024, pp. 7-8). InstructGPT's 1.3B model beating the 175B GPT-3 shows that the training approach can matter more than scale.

In QA automation and data engineering, the practical lesson is about build versus buy. Very few teams will ever pre-train a model, but every team that adopts one inherits its inference costs and its data choices. Knowing which stage drives which cost helps me ask vendors better questions and argue for fine-tuning or retrieval over training from scratch when the budget calls for it.
